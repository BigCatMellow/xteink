# Lifelong Reading Curriculum — Research and Study System v2

**Scope:** The existing 1,300 candidate entries remain available, but no book-count target controls selection. This edition adds 7 foundational/learning resources not in that proposal. **No installed XTEINK app, save data, or ebooks have been changed.**

## What is done versus provisional

- **Done:** 22 skill competencies with an acyclic prerequisite graph; six curated modules of 12 readings (72 total); 18 comparative inquiries with work references; 74 deduplicated anchor profiles; and exercise suggestions across all 867 proposed route entries.
- **Verified externally:** a set of university/publisher curriculum requirements and teaching methods (listed below). Course requirements are institution-specific: e.g., MIT 18.05 lists *multivariable calculus* as a prerequisite, while OpenStax Introductory Statistics is intended as an accessible introductory alternative. Those are **not interchangeable course levels**.
- **Not done:** full verification of every title, author, edition, translation, ownership status, scholarly relevance, or historical context across 1,300 entries. Generic discipline annotations are **heuristics**, not academic certification.
- **Inventory distinction:** Your 693 file entries were previously grouped into 625 work labels. Some omnibus items and Malazan truncated filenames remain ambiguous. Do not delete or equate files based on this plan.

## Curriculum architecture

1. **Browse/catalog:** Everything is retained. Ownership is an availability flag rather than a gate. Optional sequels and rereads stay possible.
2. **Readings and seminars:** Six 12-item foundation modules alternate enjoyable literature, historical primary texts, contemporary scholarship and accessible technical material. This is a study *spine* rather than an obligatory straight-line reading order.
3. **Skill gates:** Technical skills build on prerequisites and demonstrations. A skill gate is a way to check readiness, not a judgment about intelligence.
4. **Inquiry arcs:** Compare multiple viewpoints on a genuine question using suitable standards of evidence. Steelman views without false equivalence.
5. **Assignments and review:** Reading and retention are separate goals. Use retrieval, written argument, problem-solving, and cross-source checks.

## Compare views: guided inquiry arcs

Each inquiry should result in one piece of evidence (argument, essay, chart, calculation, or timeline), not just a checked-off reading.

### arc-01: How do we decide what counts as evidence?
**Works:** Asking the Right Questions; The Scout Mindset; The Demon-Haunted World; The Structure of Scientific Revolutions; The Logic of Scientific Discovery.
**Research question:** Reconstruct the different meanings of evidence, falsifiability, institutional practice and belief revision; do not pretend each author answers the same question.
**Assignment:** Make a premise-conclusion map, write the strongest objection and the strongest reply. **Evidence:** 400–700 words; cite two passages and label one uncertain inference.
*Caution:* The works have different eras, forms and evidence types; a fiction scene is not equivalent to a survey or an archival document.

### arc-02: Prediction, probability and causal claims
**Works:** The Art of Statistics; How to Lie with Statistics; Superforecasting; The Book of Why; Statistical Rethinking.
**Research question:** Test a numerical claim using base rates, predictions, causal assumptions and replicable calculations.
**Assignment:** Create a small dataset, graph it with units, interpret an uncertainty or denominator issue. **Evidence:** Reproducible table/chart and a 250-word limits note; solve five related problems.
*Caution:* The works have different eras, forms and evidence types; a fiction scene is not equivalent to a survey or an archival document.

### arc-03: Freedom and institutional design
**Works:** The Federalist papers; Common Sense, the Rights of Man and Other Essential; The Souls of Black Folk; The Dispossessed.
**Research question:** Compare the promises of institutions with who can participate in them, across historical and fictional contexts.
**Assignment:** Create a 6-event chronology with source types; compare two accounts of one contested claim. **Evidence:** Two primary/secondary sources and a 500-word evidence-weighted synthesis.
*Caution:* The works have different eras, forms and evidence types; a fiction scene is not equivalent to a survey or an archival document.

### arc-04: How should histories be written?
**Works:** A people's history of the United States; Battle Cry of Freedom; The Black Jacobins; Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass.
**Research question:** Separate primary testimony, historian interpretation, source coverage and contested causal judgments.
**Assignment:** Create a 6-event chronology with source types; compare two accounts of one contested claim. **Evidence:** Two primary/secondary sources and a 500-word evidence-weighted synthesis.
*Caution:* The works have different eras, forms and evidence types; a fiction scene is not equivalent to a survey or an archival document.

### arc-05: Culture, universality and psychology
**Works:** The Analects; Nicomachean Ethics; The Sociological Imagination; The WEIRDest People in the World.
**Research question:** Ask whether concepts and findings travel across cultures; evaluate translation and sampling limits.
**Assignment:** Locate one empirical claim and identify sampling, operationalization, confounding and replication concerns. **Evidence:** An annotated evidence-quality table with sources.
*Caution:* The works have different eras, forms and evidence types; a fiction scene is not equivalent to a survey or an archival document.

### arc-06: Science, society and accountability
**Works:** Silent Spring; The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks; The Selfish Gene; A Short History of Nearly Everything.
**Research question:** Analyze scientific mechanism separately from consent, policy and ethical implications.
**Assignment:** Reconstruct one explanatory model, its predictions, competing explanations and a possible falsifying observation. **Evidence:** One-page model diagram, evidence table and uncertainty note.
*Caution:* The works have different eras, forms and evidence types; a fiction scene is not equivalent to a survey or an archival document.

### arc-07: What does a literary narrator know?
**Works:** The Great Gatsby; True Grit; The Remains of the Day; Collected Ficciones.
**Research question:** Use passage-level evidence to investigate voice, knowledge, omissions and audience.
**Assignment:** Close-read two short passages for style, perspective and ambiguity; compare an alternative interpretation. **Evidence:** 600–900-word essay with direct textual evidence, not plot summary.
*Caution:* The works have different eras, forms and evidence types; a fiction scene is not equivalent to a survey or an archival document.

### arc-08: Empire and representation
**Works:** Things Fall Apart; The Black Jacobins; Beloved; The autobiography of Malcolm X.
**Research question:** Attend to different centuries, forms and experiences; do not collapse them into one colonial narrative.
**Assignment:** Create a 6-event chronology with source types; compare two accounts of one contested claim. **Evidence:** Two primary/secondary sources and a 500-word evidence-weighted synthesis.
*Caution:* The works have different eras, forms and evidence types; a fiction scene is not equivalent to a survey or an archival document.

### arc-09: Learning what mathematics can prove
**Works:** Flatland; Euclid's Elements; How to Prove It; OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e.
**Research question:** Demonstrate one proof and identify where an analogy is not a proof.
**Assignment:** Solve 5–10 assigned problems without looking at solutions; then correct and explain errors. **Evidence:** Worked steps, answer checks and an error log; 80% independent success before advancing.
*Caution:* The works have different eras, forms and evidence types; a fiction scene is not equivalent to a survey or an archival document.

### arc-10: How do we know that a policy worked?
**Works:** Poor Economics; Why Nations Fail; The Art of Statistics; The Economy 2.0: Microeconomics.
**Research question:** Differentiate randomized effects, mechanisms, institutions, external validity and confounding.
**Assignment:** Map actors, constraints, causal claims and distributional effects of a proposed policy. **Evidence:** One model or chart, evidence against own view, and a 600-word tradeoff memo.
*Caution:* The works have different eras, forms and evidence types; a fiction scene is not equivalent to a survey or an archival document.

### arc-11: Modern dystopias as questions, not predictions

### arc-12: Religion, morality and responsibility

**Assignment:** Evaluate one AI result against a baseline; identify dataset biases, failure modes and uncertainty. **Evidence:** Repeatable mini-evaluation with rubric and limitations.
*Caution:* The works have different eras, forms and evidence types; a fiction scene is not equivalent to a survey or an archival document.

### arc-14: Independent interdisciplinary capstone
**Works:** The Craft of Research; The Sociological Imagination; The Book of Why; Beloved.

## Context and original study prompts for selected anchors

This section is a curated starting point, not the verified contents of the ebook editions. Context is distinct from interpretation.

**The Great Gatsby** — United States, Jazz Age and post-WWI wealth, 1925.
- Question: How do narrated desire and class shape the American Dream?
- Exercise: Compare Nick's descriptions of Gatsby in three scenes; identify a possible unreliable judgment.

**1984** — Post-WWII dystopian novel, published 1949.
- Question: How does control of language and memory affect political domination?
- Exercise: Compare fictional mechanisms with a documented historical practice without equating them.

**The Dispossessed** — 1974 speculative fiction amid Cold War ideological debate.
- Question: Which social arrangements trade off freedom, equality and solidarity?
- Exercise: Separate the novel's observations from empirical claims; construct a comparison table.

**Asking the Right Questions** — Contemporary critical-reasoning textbook.
- Question: What makes a reason relevant to a conclusion?
- Exercise: Map a claim from a newspaper editorial into premises and unstated assumptions.

**The Scout Mindset** — Contemporary writing on identity and belief revision.
- Question: What changes when we treat beliefs as hypotheses rather than loyalties?
- Exercise: Document one changed prediction and the evidence that changed it.

**Superforecasting** — 21st-century probabilistic judgment research.
- Question: What distinguishes a measurable forecast from a vague prediction?
- Exercise: Write five dated probabilistic predictions and specify scoring/updates.

**How to Lie with Statistics** — Popular 1950s critique of misleading statistical presentation.
- Question: How do displays influence interpretation of evidence?
- Exercise: Find a current graph and redraw it with clear labels, denominators and uncertainty.

**The Logic of Scientific Discovery** — 20th-century philosophy of science; 1930s original publication.
- Question: What would make a scientific theory falsifiable?
- Exercise: Compare Popper and Kuhn using one live research controversy.

**The Book of Why** — Contemporary causal inference popular science.
- Question: Why does causal inference require assumptions beyond correlation?
- Exercise: Draw three causal diagrams, explain each confounder and possible intervention.

**How to Prove It** — Contemporary introduction to logical proof writing.
- Question: What makes a mathematical claim demonstrably true?
- Exercise: Write and check three short proofs using different methods.

**Statistical Rethinking** — Modern Bayesian applied statistical modeling text.
- Question: How do model assumptions shape uncertainty and causal interpretations?
- Exercise: Complete one model fitting exercise, posterior check and sensitivity comparison.

**The Prince** — Renaissance Italy's fragmented polities, early 16th century.
- Question: When does political survival conflict with ordinary morality?
- Exercise: Test a proposed maxim against one historical case and its counterexample.

**The Idea of Justice** — Contemporary comparative approach to justice, Amartya Sen 2009.
- Question: Can we reduce manifest injustice without agreeing on perfect institutions?
- Exercise: Compare two policies against measurable capabilities and rights tradeoffs.

**The Economy 2.0: Microeconomics** — Contemporary economics textbook covering incentives, institutions and outcomes.
- Question: How do rules and bargaining power shape gains from exchange?
- Exercise: Use a model and real-world data to analyze a rule change, with distributional results.

**Battle Cry of Freedom** — Late-20th-century synthesis of US Civil War era scholarship.
- Question: What causes a war and how do historians weigh competing causes?
- Exercise: Write a claim-evidence table comparing two explanations of secession.

**The Selfish Gene** — 1970s popular evolutionary synthesis emphasizing gene-level selection.
- Question: What explanatory power and limits follow from a gene-centered model?
- Exercise: Distinguish the scientific model from moral/metaphorical interpretations.

**The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks** — Contemporary science history and ethics covering mid-20th-century human cell research.
- Question: How do consent, power and scientific benefit intersect?
- Exercise: Map stakeholders and create an ethics protocol with realistic tradeoffs.

**The WEIRDest People in the World** — Contemporary cultural-evolutionary psychology published 2020.
- Question: How representative are psychological findings from different populations?
- Exercise: Evaluate a generalization by checking sampling, alternative explanations and replication.

**The Elements of Computing Systems** — 21st-century computer-science education from hardware to software.
- Question: How can complex computation emerge from simple abstractions?
- Exercise: Build and test a small logical component, then explain its abstraction boundary.

**Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach** — Modern university-level AI textbook covering search, reasoning, learning and agents.
- Question: Which problem formulations explain AI capability and limitation?
- Exercise: Implement or simulate one algorithm and compare with a trivial baseline.

**The Republic** — Plato's classical Greek philosophical dialogue, 4th century BCE.
- Question: What makes a political arrangement just, and for whom?
- Exercise: Map one Socratic argument, then write an objection from a different conception of justice.

**Nicomachean Ethics** — Classical Greek ethics, Aristotle, 4th century BCE.
- Question: What role do habits and virtues play in living well?
- Exercise: Define one virtue and compare virtue-based and rule-based responses to a case.

**On Liberty** — 19th-century British liberalism and public debate.
- Question: When should society constrain an individual's conduct?
- Exercise: Apply Mill's principle to a case, then develop the strongest limitation.

**Second Treatise of Government** — Late-17th-century English political thought amid constitutional conflict.
- Question: What grounds legitimate political authority and property rights?
- Exercise: Reconstruct Locke's argument, then consider a historically grounded challenge.

**The Souls of Black Folk** — US post-Reconstruction Black political/social thought, published 1903.
- Question: How do power, education and identity shape citizenship?
- Exercise: Compare a Du Bois claim with historical legislation or census evidence.

**The Analects** — Early Chinese Confucian sayings compiled over time.
- Question: How do learning, ritual and relationships cultivate character?
- Exercise: Compare an Analects passage with Aristotle without assuming identical terms.

**Poor Economics** — Contemporary evidence-based development economics published 2011.
- Question: What can field experiments establish about development policy?
- Exercise: Identify randomization, effect sizes, external validity and ethical limitations.

**Why Nations Fail** — Contemporary institutional-development thesis published 2012.
- Question: How strong is the evidence linking institutions with long-run prosperity?
- Exercise: Contrast the thesis with geographic, colonial and state-capacity accounts using country cases.

**The Art of Statistics** — Contemporary statistical-literacy and inference text.
- Question: How can a numerical finding mislead even when calculated correctly?
- Exercise: Recreate one example using explicit denominator and sampling assumptions.

**The Structure of Scientific Revolutions** — Philosophy/history of science during 1960s postwar scientific expansion.
- Question: What counts as progress when research frameworks change?
- Exercise: Compare Kuhn's view with a real research change; identify evidence that complicates the story.

**The Demon-Haunted World** — Popular scientific skepticism published in 1995.
- Question: What separates repeatable evidence from persuasive narratives?
- Exercise: Analyze a debatable claim using alternative hypotheses and source credibility.

**Silent Spring** — 1962 environmental science and public-policy intervention.
- Question: How should scientific evidence inform regulation under uncertainty?
- Exercise: Identify evidence and counterclaims in the original debate and compare with current findings.

**The Black Jacobins** — 1930s anti-colonial historical account of the Haitian Revolution.
- Question: How does a revolutionary history change when enslaved people are its agents?
- Exercise: Compare sources about one event; discuss the historian's political location.

**The Craft of Research** — Contemporary academic research and writing methods.
- Question: How does a question become a defensible claim?
- Exercise: Create a research question, evidence plan and annotated bibliography.

**The Sociological Imagination** — Mid-20th-century sociology, published 1959.
- Question: How do biographies connect to institutions and historical change?
- Exercise: Explain one individual problem in structural terms; test against data.

**Things Fall Apart** — 1958 Nigerian novel reflecting on Igbo society and colonial encounter.
- Question: Whose perspective has authority when cultures meet?
- Exercise: Contrast a scene's interior perspective with a colonial-era description of similar practices.

**Beloved** — 1987 American novel examining slavery and cultural memory.
- Question: What narrative techniques express the afterlives of historical trauma?
- Exercise: Use close reading and separate literary interpretation from historical documentation.

**A Room of One’s Own** — 1929 feminist literary criticism in interwar Britain.
- Question: How do material conditions shape whose work becomes canonical?
- Exercise: Compare Woolf's argument with documented publishing and education access.

## Assignment and assessment standard

### A short close-reading seminar (45–75 minutes)

1. Read a manageable passage with no summarizer. Mark two ambiguous lines and one assumption.
2. Without looking, write a 3-sentence explanation of the thesis or central tension.
3. Reopen and correct your account; distinguish what the author says from what you infer.
4. Reconstruct a strong alternative interpretation from the text itself.
5. Write 300–600 words with direct evidence, and keep your unresolved question.

### A research-text cycle (1–3 sessions)

1. Identify research question, design, sample, measurements, and key inference.
2. Find independent research or primary data; check date, incentives, and replication.
3. Recreate a chart, calculation or argument diagram (show assumptions and units).
4. State strongest objection and what new evidence would change the conclusion.

### A technical-text cycle

1. Do a diagnostic problem before choosing the chapter.
2. Work 5–10 problems without looking at worked solutions.
3. Explain each mistake, then solve analogous problems a few days later.
4. For calculations, label units, denominators and uncertainty; for code, add tests.
5. Advance for demonstrated understanding, not total pages read.

### Gentle pacing and spaced recall

- Each 4-reading block may be 1 enjoyable title, 1 primary/interpretive text, 1 evidence-based nonfiction, plus 1 technical or writing assignment. There is no calendar quota.
- Use a 5-minute no-notes recall after each substantial session, revisit after a few days and again weeks later. Log errors and revised beliefs rather than just highlights.
- Keep unfinished series optional, and reread favorites without marking them as new knowledge.

## Progress and evaluation

| Dimension | Evidence to retain |
|---|---|
| Coverage | Disciplines encountered, including global history, non-Western traditions and quantitative methods |
| Interpretation | Passages analyzed and alternative readings considered |
| Argument | Accurate reconstructed arguments plus objections and revisions |
| Quantitative | Independent problems solved and mistakes corrected |
| Sources | Primary/secondary source provenance, citations, uncertainty and contradiction |
| Synthesis | A 1,000–1,500-word essay or reproducible mini-project at each module ending |
| Enjoyment | Personal interest and rereads; never grade it as academic achievement |

## Known source and research limitations

- *Academic stature* varies by discipline: works can be historically influential but empirically outdated, harmful, or wrong. Read them to understand arguments, not to adopt them.
- Philosophical and political texts are **not** assigned equal empirical validity. State each tradition accurately and adjudicate factual claims with appropriate sources.
- Several works are *selections*, *course packs*, or *textbooks*; the 1,300 count is a catalog-entry count, not a promise of 1,300 complete physical books.
- For translated primary texts, record translator/edition before detailed interpretation. For science/statistics, prioritize current methods and corrections.
- Proposed book-level context is not based on reading every source. Check editions and scholarly introductions before asserting precise textual claims.
- The review queue CSV enumerates all catalog entries whose bibliography/edition still needs checking. The JSON preserves original work IDs so it can be integrated without resetting reading progress.
